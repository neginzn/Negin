CONTENT WARNING
This episode discusses the online abuse of children and suicide. No images of the victims' final moments are shown. If you're struggling, resources are in the description.

CH 1 — The Phone
January 17th, 2022. Gig Harbor, Washington. Early morning. Police find the body of a 13-year-old boy behind a supermarket, about a fifteen-minute walk from his home. His phone is lying on the ground nearby. His name is Jay Taylor. For the first few days, nothing suggests anyone else was involved. It looks like a suicide. What the police don't know yet is that Jay wasn't alone. People in several countries were watching him. Live. And according to prosecutors, the person running that call was more than five thousand miles away, in Hamburg, Germany. He was 17 years old. And Jay wasn't the first. Prosecutors say there were more than 30 children, in five countries.

CH 2 — The Door
During the pandemic, Jay's parents realized something was wrong. Their son was struggling, and they found out he'd been spending time in dark corners of the internet. Online groups where kids talked about hurting themselves. Jay had only recently told them he was transgender, and they stood by him. But they also acted fast. Doctors. Therapists. Strict limits on his phone: one hour a day, only in the living room, where they could see him. And by the end of 2021, it seemed to be working. Jay was getting better. But there was one door his parents didn't know about. After midnight, his time limit reset. And Jay went online. Alone. With no one watching.

CH 3 — Eva
The first person to reach Jay that night wasn't the man in Hamburg. It was a girl from Finland. We'll call her Eva. She was around twelve years old. And she was a victim too. For months, someone online had made her feel loved. Then he started threatening her. He had her photos. Her personal information. Every time she tried to leave, he pulled her back. He wanted one thing from her that she refused to give. Her life. So, investigators believe, she found him someone else.

CH 4 — 2:01 AM
The night of January 16th, Jay posts a message on Discord, in a group for lonely teenagers. He's looking for a friend. Someone his age to talk to about music and crochet. Preferably someone LGBTQ, like him. At 2:01 in the morning, Eva messages him. When the conversation turns to suicide, Jay says no. Then he says it again. He sends more than thirty messages. He lists the things he wants to stay alive for. He apologizes. Then Eva tells him she wants to die too, and that they could do it together. About half an hour later, Jay agrees. Even then, in one of his last messages, he writes that he'd rather it didn't happen. His mother is asleep a few feet away. She's been sleeping near him every night to keep him safe. Jay walks past her, and out of the house.

CH 5 — White Tiger
Outside, the conversation moves to an Instagram group. And Eva invites someone in. He joins with a hacked account that belongs to a girl in California. But online, they know him by another name. White Tiger. Prosecutors say that from that moment, he takes control of the call. More people join. They watch. And minutes later, Jay Taylor ends his life on a livestream. In the messages that survived, some of the people watching start to mock him. The recording is saved. Within hours, it's being passed around.

CH 6 — The Message
White Tiger thinks it's over. The video is out there, passed from account to account, inside a network built to stay hidden. What he doesn't know is that someone in that group is saving everything. Six days after Jay's death. The middle of the night. A message arrives for Jay's parents. The sender is a stranger. A teenage girl, on the other side of the world, in Australia. And she's not an outsider. The same people have been targeting her too. She was in the group when they shared the video. She saw them laughing at it. And she decided she couldn't stay silent. Quietly, she saves the messages. She saves the usernames. Then she finds a way to reach Jay's father, Colby. Her message is short. There's a video. You need to see it. Colby takes the phone into the bathroom and locks the door, so his wife won't see. He presses play. A group call. Only one camera on. He recognizes the black and white sweater immediately. It's his son. He knows what's coming. He stops the video before it gets there. Along with the video, the girl sends a list of usernames. And an apology, for making him see it. Colby takes everything to the police. Until that night, they had a suicide. Now, they had names.
🎬 [ABC News interview — Colby / Leslie Taylor]

CH 7 — The Man in Hamburg
The name behind White Tiger, according to prosecutors, is Shahriar J. German-Iranian. His family was wealthy. His father built a successful medical technology company. A former classmate described him as shy. That's what we know about Shahriar J. Plus one more detail. He was studying to become a doctor. Prosecutors say his online life looked very different. They say he followed the same pattern with every victim. First, he made them feel loved. Then he pushed them to hurt themselves on camera, and used those videos to force them into worse. The victims identified so far were between 11 and 15 years old. From Germany, the UK, Canada, Finland, and the United States. One of them, a girl in Canada, believed he was her boyfriend. She attempted suicide, but she survived.

CH 8 — The Warnings
And here's the part of this story that's hardest to explain. They knew. In July 2021, months before Jay died, American authorities flagged illegal content linked to a teenager in Hamburg to German federal police. A few months later, Shahriar walked into a police station with his mother. He admitted to having a small amount of illegal material. He wasn't arrested. In February 2023, the FBI gave German police his identity. They had his name. Later that year, his home was searched. According to German media, investigators found hundreds of these recordings. Some of the officers who reviewed them needed psychological counseling to keep going. They took his phone and his computer. But they didn't take him. He bought new devices. And according to investigators, he kept going. For almost two more years.

CH 9 — The Arrest
June 17th, 2025. Hamburg police enter his parents' home in the middle of the night. They're hoping he's awake, with a device unlocked, so they can get into his encrypted files. He's asleep. But this time, they take him. Twenty-two investigators and four prosecutors work the case. Analyzing a single 75-minute chat takes them seventeen working days.

CH 10 — The Number
January 9th, 2026. Hamburg. Almost four years after Jay's death, Shahriar J. finally walks into a courtroom. And then the doors close. Because he was a minor when it started, the public isn't allowed in. Whatever happens inside stays inside. 204 charges. One count of murder. Five of attempted murder. More than 30 children. His lawyer calls the charges "experimental" and "not provable." And Shahriar? He says nothing. But the biggest shock in this case isn't inside the courtroom. It's in the law. Under juvenile law, even if he's convicted on every single count, the maximum sentence is ten years in a youth detention facility. Ten years. At most. And there's one more problem. In Germany, suicide isn't a crime. And helping someone die isn't automatically murder, if that person made the choice freely. So to convict him of murder, prosecutors have to prove one thing. That Jay never really chose. That a 13-year-old boy, who said no more than thirty times, was used as a tool in someone else's hands.

CH 11 — Waiting
The court has scheduled 82 sessions, running until the end of 2026. Until a verdict, Shahriar J. is presumed innocent. And whatever that verdict is, it can only measure one person. Not the platforms. Not the warnings that were ignored. Not the network that's still out there. In February 2026, Jay's parents sued Discord. They allege the platform was warned months before their son died. Jay's parents did everything right. And it still wasn't enough. So tell me: is ten years enough? Write it in the comments. I'll be following this case until the verdict.
