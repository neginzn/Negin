CONTENT WARNING
This episode discusses the online abuse of children and suicide. No images of the victims' final moments are shown. If you're struggling, resources are in the description.

CH 1 — The Phone
January 17th, 2022. Gig Harbor, Washington. Early morning. Police find the body of a 13-year-old boy behind a supermarket, about a fifteen-minute walk from his home. His phone is lying on the ground nearby. His name is Jay Taylor. For the first few days, nothing suggests anyone else was involved. It looks like a suicide. What the police don't know yet is that Jay wasn't alone. People in several countries were watching him. Live. And according to prosecutors, the person running that call was hundreds of miles away, in Hamburg, Germany. He was 17 years old.

CH 2 — The Boy Who Knitted
Jay was the kind of kid who made things for people. Bracelets with his family's names on them. A half-finished pink sweater for his aunt. He and his mom even ran a small online shop to sell what he made. When he was ten, Jay told his mother he was a boy. During the pandemic, stuck at home, he started to struggle. His parents did everything they could. Doctors. Therapists. Treatment centers in three states. They limited his phone to one hour a day, only in the living room. And by the end of 2021, Jay seemed better. He was excited to go back to school. That's everything his parents knew. Well, almost everything. Because the one-hour limit reset every night. At midnight.

CH 3 — Eva
The first person to reach Jay that night wasn't the man in Hamburg. It was a girl from Finland. We'll call her Eva. She was around twelve years old. And she was a victim too. For months, someone online had made her feel loved. Then he started threatening her. He had her photos. Her personal information. Every time she tried to leave, he pulled her back. He wanted one thing from her that she refused to give. Her life. So, investigators believe, she found him someone else.

CH 4 — 2:01 AM
The night of January 16th, Jay posts a message on Discord, in a group for lonely teenagers. He writes: "I'm looking for friends, preferably LGBTQ, for crochet buddies." At 2:01 in the morning, Eva messages him. When the conversation turns to suicide, Jay says no. Then he says it again. He sends more than thirty messages. He lists the things he wants to stay alive for. He apologizes. Then Eva tells him she wants to die too, and that they could do it together. About half an hour later, Jay agrees. Even then, in one of his last messages, he writes that he'd rather it didn't happen. His mother is asleep a few feet away. She's been sleeping near him every night to keep him safe. Jay walks past her, and out of the house.

CH 5 — White Tiger
Outside, the conversation moves to an Instagram group. And Eva invites someone in. He joins with a hacked account that belongs to a girl in California. But online, they know him by another name. White Tiger. Prosecutors say that from that moment, he takes control of the call. More people join. They watch. And minutes later, Jay Taylor ends his life on a livestream. In the messages that survived, some of the people watching start to mock him. The recording is saved. Within hours, it's being passed around.

CH 6 — The Message
White Tiger probably thought this would stay where everything else stayed. Inside the network. He was wrong. Six days later, in the middle of the night, Jay's parents receive a message from a stranger. A teenage girl in Australia, more than seven thousand miles away. She had been targeted by the same people. And she had been in the group where they were sharing the video, laughing at it. She saved everything. Then she found a way to reach Jay's father, Colby. She told him there was a video he needed to see. Colby takes the phone into the bathroom and locks the door, so his wife won't see it. He recognizes his son's sweater immediately. He knows what's coming. He stops the video before it gets there. Along with the video, the girl sends a list of usernames. And an apology, for making him see it.
🎬 [ABC News interview — Colby / Leslie Taylor]

CH 7 — The Man in Hamburg
The name behind White Tiger, according to prosecutors, is Shahriar J. German-Iranian. His family was wealthy. His father built a successful medical technology company. A former classmate described him as shy. That's what we know about Shahriar J. Plus one more detail. He was studying to become a doctor. Prosecutors say his online life looked very different. They say he followed the same pattern with every victim. First, he made them feel loved. Then he pushed them to hurt themselves on camera, and used those videos to force them into worse. The victims identified so far were between 11 and 15 years old. From Germany, the UK, Canada, Finland, and the United States. One of them, a girl in Canada, believed he was her boyfriend. She attempted suicide. She survived.

CH 8 — The Warnings
And here's the part of this story that's hardest to explain. They knew. In July 2021, months before Jay died, American authorities flagged illegal content linked to a teenager in Hamburg to German federal police. A few months later, Shahriar walked into a police station with his mother. He admitted to having a small amount of illegal material. He wasn't arrested. In February 2023, the FBI gave German police his identity. They had his name. Later that year, his home was searched. According to German media, investigators found hundreds of these recordings. Some of the officers who reviewed them needed psychological counseling to keep going. They took his phone and his computer. But they didn't take him. He bought new devices. And according to investigators, he kept going. For almost two more years.

CH 9 — The Arrest
June 17th, 2025. Hamburg police enter his parents' home in the middle of the night. They're hoping he's awake, with a device unlocked, so they can get into his encrypted files. He's asleep. But this time, they take him. Twenty-two investigators and four prosecutors work the case. Analyzing a single 75-minute chat takes them seventeen working days.

CH 10 — The Number
January 9th, 2026. Almost four years after Jay's death, the trial of Shahriar J. begins in Hamburg. 204 charges. Including one count of murder and five of attempted murder. More than 30 children. His lawyer calls the charges "experimental" and "not provable." And because he was a minor when it started, the trial is closed to the public, and it falls under juvenile law. Which means that even if he's convicted on every single count, the sentence is between six months and ten years in a youth detention facility. Ten years. At most. There's another problem. In Germany, suicide isn't a crime. And helping someone die isn't automatically murder, if that person made the choice freely. So prosecutors have to prove that Jay's choice was never free. That a 13-year-old boy, who said no more than thirty times, was used as an instrument.

CH 11 — Waiting
The court has scheduled 82 sessions, running until the end of 2026. Until a verdict, Shahriar J. is presumed innocent. And whatever that verdict is, it can only measure one person. Not the platforms. Not the warnings that were ignored. Not the network that's still out there. In February 2026, Jay's parents sued Discord. They say the platform was warned months before their son died. Jay's parents did everything right. And it still wasn't enough. So tell me: is ten years enough? Write it in the comments. I'll be following this case until the verdict.
