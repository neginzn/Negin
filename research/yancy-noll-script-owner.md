THE MAN WHO KILLED FOR THE THRILL

CH 1 — Ten Minutes After Work · 0:00
This is Yancy Noll. He lives in Seattle, works at a QFC, and he's a wine specialist. Sometimes he goes hiking with his girlfriend, sometimes with his dog, sometimes alone. That's everything we know about this 42-year-old man. Well — almost everything. There's one more thing.
August 31st, 2012. Exactly ten minutes after he clocked out, driving his old Subaru home, he stops at a red light. A silver Z4 pulls up next to him. By the time Yancy turns his head, he's dead. Four bullets.
The people at the scene heard the shots and ran — but they saw the killer too. One of them even tried to chase the car and couldn't catch it. He only got the driver in profile. And from that, with the police, he produced this sketch.

CH 2 — The Window Was Up · 1:15
Then the crime scene report lands, and it says something that changes everything. The victim's windows were down. But the shooter fired five rounds from behind his own wheel, without ever rolling down his passenger window. The first shot missed. Every one after that went straight into the victim's temple. Which means, very likely, this man was trained.
So here's everything Seattle PD has: a few seconds of security camera footage, a sketch of a profile, and a silver Z4 with a shattered passenger window. They push all of it out across the whole region — not just to officers, but to the public, so somebody out there might close the gap.

CH 3 — The Least Likely Suspect · 2:15
Two weeks go by. September 14th, a woman calls the police to report something suspicious. To her, that sketch is a man named Dinh Bowman, who lives less than ten blocks from where Yancy was killed. Everything lines up — the hair, the bone structure, all of it.
But Dinh Bowman is the least likely suspect in this entire case. He's a 29-year-old engineer. A prodigy. He started college at twelve. He started his own company at twenty. He has a list of inventions behind his name. In 2007, at a training seminar, he met a woman named Jennifer Palm — a successful, well-known dentist. A year later they were married.
That's what we know about Dinh Bowman. Plus one more detail. Dinh's car is a silver Z4.

CH 4 — The Car in the Garage · 3:25
Police sit on the house for a few days. No sign of the car. Then, on September 21st, the garage door opens once — and the car has been sitting inside the entire time. Officers get a search warrant immediately, and while Dinh and Jennifer are at work, they tow the Z4 out of the garage.
Now it's time to talk to the prime suspect. That same evening, Dinh Bowman is arrested and taken into an interrogation room.

CH 5 — Two Hours Alone · 4:10
The police leave him alone in there for about two hours. It's a classic interrogation technique — the idea is to drag the wait out until the subject is right at the edge of what he can tolerate, and then take a confession with one small push. A lot of the time, it genuinely works. But not here. Dinh spends the whole two hours eating snacks and drinking, and he's confident enough to tell the officers straight out that they're wasting his time.
🎬 [interrogation clip — Dinh]
Dinh works it out fast: a lawyer who knows the law and gives the detective the absolute minimum beats wrestling the system himself. What the subject doesn't know is how much evidence they already have on him.
Reading his behavior isn't hard. He's completely composed. He holds eye contact. He asks the detective questions instead of answering them. He volunteers nothing — he asks for explanations.

CH 6 — The Room Next Door · 5:40
And while he's sitting there being read his charges, he has no idea that in the room next door, his wife Jennifer is being interrogated too.

Before we look at Jennifer's interview, one thing worth saying. According to the people around them, these two weren't just in love — they were extremely close, they understood each other, and both of them were highly intelligent. Jennifer doesn't ask for a lawyer like her husband did. But in her own way, she picks an interesting escape route.
🎬 [interrogation clip — Jennifer, "I'm not sure"]

CH 7 — What She Didn't Know · 6:45
There are two things she doesn't know. First, the police have already identified Dinh's car. Second — this mark right here. Checking it told the officers the passenger window had been replaced. And remember: the killer fired from behind the wheel with his window up. On top of that, when they searched the inside of the car, they found broken glass down beside the seat. And the officers who went into the garage to move the vehicle mentioned a smell of paint in their report — the wheels had been resprayed black.
Detectives believed she knew more than she was saying — but she was never charged with any crime.

CH 8 — The Portland Story · 7:35
Here's the story she tells about that trip. She says on September 1st — the day after the shooting — they drove to Portland, and while they were stopped somewhere for lunch, the car got broken into. That's why the window was smashed. Afterwards they went and had it replaced.
Then the police go through her things. And the receipts tell a different order. They replaced the window first. They sat down at the restaurant around seven in the evening.
Then there's the second trip, about a week later, when they decided to drive north of Seattle and swap the car's expensive tires for a cheap set.

CH 9 — "You Need to Grow Up, Lady" · 8:35
And notice the contrast. However calm and self-assured Dinh is in the other room, Jennifer is agitated, avoiding the detective's eyes — and that alone is enough to make the police suspicious. The interview apparently ran four hours. The full video isn't available, or at least I couldn't find it. But there's one important stretch, where the detective drops every classic technique, sees the subject wavering, and goes straight at her.
🎬 "...you're a smart woman trying to be the good wife and protecting this guy — OK, when he's murdering people. This guy died. He's dead. He's got a girlfriend, he's got family, and they all freaking cry every day. We have them in this very same room. And you're going to sit in here and protect him? That is sick. That is disgusting. You need to grow up, lady."

CH 10 — An Empty House · 9:25
When the interrogation ends, officers go into the suspect's house. And the house is empty. No furniture. No bed. Just a mattress and a few things on the floor. The only thing the search adds to the case file is a handful of guns and ammunition — but not the 9mm the police are actually looking for.
Bail is set at around ten million dollars, which he can't pay. According to the reports, he shows no sign of remorse or fear in that courtroom. Same as the interrogation room — completely calm, completely self-righteous.

CH 11 — The Tutorial · 10:25
Then the investigation moves to Dinh Bowman's personal computer. And it's full of videos: stunt driving practice, professional-level shooting. But one video, from a firearms specialist, is the one that catches the detectives' attention. Because that clip is the exact blueprint of what happened the day Yancy Noll died. Dinh followed the tutorial so precisely that he didn't even roll his passenger window down.

CH 12 — The Missing Motive · 11:05
With that video added to everything else, almost every question in this case has an answer. Except one. Motive.

The prosecutor's read was this: one morning Dinh Bowman woke up, and after all that training and preparation, he told himself today's the day. And he probably did it for one big rush. That's why he did it in broad daylight — so there would be witnesses, so there would be a chase, so he'd have to run, and so he'd finally have to hide his car. Every one of those things pushes the thrill higher.

CH 13 — Murder, and James Bond · 11:50
There's another part of this case worth hearing: the hundreds of phone calls between Dinh and his wife Jennifer. The detectives said they had never heard conversations like this from any inmate.
🎬 "How are you? I'm doing good... I miss so many things. I don't have a snuggle... love love love love love."
While those two are on the phone with each other, the detectives keep digging through the computer. And Dinh's system has a large collection of films, videos and books about one specific subject. Murder. He also had an intense interest in one particular man. James Bond.
After police break into his personal machine, they find something else. Right after the shooting, Dinh went to his computer, opened a folder full of books about guns and killing, and read one of them. A book with very useful information about not getting arrested — how to hide a murder, how to get rid of the weapon, what to do about bullet fragments.
And it looks like reading that is what pushed him to erase even the smallest traces. The tires, for example. In the moment he fled, he felt like he'd left rubber on the asphalt. So a few days later he changed the tires and sprayed the rims black, so they wouldn't even look the same.
Thanks to that manual, the gun was never found. But with the old tires, the shooting-competition footage and everything after it, the police had more than enough to charge Dinh Bowman with the murder of Yancy Noll.

CH 14 — Self-Defense · 13:55
And look at how his face changes in court. He now looks like a well-behaved thirteen-year-old student. Which, by the way, isn't unusual at all — plenty of defendants add a layer of innocence to their appearance hoping it lands on the judge.
🎬 "...just this crazy bad dream, like I was running from a monster... the phrase that caught my attention was, 'you better learn how to drive that fancy car, boy, or you're going to get yourself—' and that's when I got — yeah, I'd describe it as just violent hatred."
In court, Dinh tells a brand-new version of that day. He says he and the victim got into a verbal fight on the road. And the second they stopped at the red light, Yancy threw a wine bottle at him that hit him square in the head. And he says he panicked — afraid the other man might have a gun — so he got there first and fired. In other words: self-defense.
There are a lot of holes in that statement. First, he says he threw the wine bottle and the rest of it away after leaving the scene — while a man with his intelligence knows perfectly well that keeping that evidence is exactly what would have made his story believable. Then he says he wasn't shooting to kill — while everyone knows he's a trained marksman, and it's very hard to believe he put four rounds into a man's temple by accident.
Over three sessions, the prosecutor took apart the entire story Dinh had spent two years building with his lawyer.

On December 9th, 2014, the jury found Dinh Bowman guilty. In January 2015, he was sentenced to 29 years and one month. And watch his face and his posture one more time.
Jennifer, who never attended a single one of her husband's court dates, later changed her name, filed for divorce, started a new life, and permanently cut every road that led back to her old one — and to the man she once loved.
